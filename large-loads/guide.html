Data centers, the grid, and the rules Texas and New York are writing for them.

By Daniel Golliher · adapted from his essay in Maximum New York, July 27, 2026

Data center policy is grid policy.

Data centers touch tax revenue, land use, water, and noise. But the core questions are about the grid: the system that generates electricity, carries it long distances on high-voltage lines, and it to homes and businesses.

The grid can only deliver so much at once.

At any moment there is a maximum amount of power the system can supply. Operators watch the gap between that ceiling and what everyone is drawing. Call it . In this drawing, a region with 10,000 of capacity is using 8,000.

Then a data center asks to plug in.

A 500 MW campus draws about as much power as 625,000 New York homes do on average, and it runs around the clock. Here it eats a quarter of the region’s slack before a single heatwave arrives.

That one hookup raises a stack of questions.

To evaluate whether, when, and how a state can accommodate data centers, you need a functional grip on all of this. Many people trip right at the starting line.

It starts with one technical idea: the large load.

Before any of those questions get answered, a state has to decide which customers count as “large loads” and what rules they face.

What counts as “large”?

A large load is any customer that draws a lot of electricity at one site: a data center, a factory, maybe even a fully electrified skyscraper.

Because data centers need so much power, they fall under most jurisdictions’ definitions. And because they are the most common kind of large load asking to connect to most grids, people use “data center” and “large load” almost interchangeably. This guide does too.

But the line itself moves. Texas passed a law in 2025, , that regulates large loads above 75 megawatts.3 New York’s Governor Hochul issued an in July 2026 that temporarily bans data centers of 50 megawatts or more, “we’ll see how temporarily.”4 The New York order exempts uses like manufacturing, research, and education. Parts of SB6’s curtailment rules exempt critical industrial customers and critical natural gas facilities.6

Megawatts measure power, a rate. They describe how hard something pulls on the grid at a single moment. Energy is that rate over time, measured in . Grids have to be built for the moment of greatest pull, which is why large-load rules draw their lines in megawatts.

Phantom loads

Six real projects, 2,000 MW.

Someone building a data center faces a variable landscape: many utilities, each with its own timeline and compliance burden for . If every developer applied once, the planner’s forecast would match reality.

So developers apply everywhere at once.

Faced with that patchwork, a developer will sometimes apply to several utilities for the same data center and see which moves fastest. For one business, that makes sense. Applicants generally haven’t told utilities that a request is duplicated elsewhere.

And long shots get in line too.

Under the old rules, a request was essentially free. Projects with little chance of panning out joined the alongside the real ones, shown here with dashed outlines.

The planner sees 12,000 MW. Reality is 2,000.

Central grid planners see each request as unique and add them up. The result is an artificially high forecast that warps long-term planning and capital investment: the power plants and transmission lines that take years to build.

Make them disclose duplicates.

Require every potential large customer to say whether it is pursuing a substantially similar request elsewhere. Planners can then count each project once. Texas’s SB6 does exactly this.

Make them put skin in the game.

Require proof of , an upfront study fee, a financial commitment, and an early-termination agreement. Most long shots withdraw. One stays and pays, which is fine: the forecast now tracks the projects that are serious.

Disclose duplicate requests

Texas SB6 · §2 · Utilities Code §37.0561(d)
The standards must require each large load customer subject to Subsection (c) to disclose to the interconnecting electric utility or municipally owned utility whether the customer is pursuing a substantially similar request for electric service in this state the approval of which would result in the customer materially changing, delaying, or withdrawing the interconnection request. The disclosure may withhold or anonymize competitively sensitive details.

Require skin in the game

Texas SB6 · §2 · study fee, site control, financial commitment

The standards must set a flat study fee of at least $100,000 to be paid to the interconnecting electric utility or municipally owned utility for initial transmission screening studies for large loads subject to Subsection (c). A large load customer that requests additional capacity following the screening study must pay an additional study fee based on the new request.

The standards must include a method for a large load customer subject to Subsection (c) to demonstrate site control for the proposed load location through an ownership interest, lease, or another legal interest acceptable to the commission.

The standards must include uniform financial commitment requirements for the development of transmission infrastructure needed to serve a large load customer subject to Subsection (c). The standards must provide that satisfactory proof of financial commitment may include: (1) security provided on a dollar per megawatt basis as set by the commission; (2) contribution in aid of construction; (3) security provided under an agreement that requires a large load customer to pay for significant equipment or services in advance of signing an agreement to establish electric delivery service; or (4) a form of financial commitment acceptable to the commission other than those provided by Subdivisions (1)-(3).

The RSVP problem. This is a scaled-up version of a familiar problem with public events. If they’re free, people say they’re coming on a whim, and many don’t show. If you charge for tickets, people tend to say they’re coming only if they mean it. Skin-in-the-game rules turn free RSVPs into tickets.

The early-termination piece guards against a second problem: , like a transmission line built for a project that quit, which ends up going to no one.

When the grid is stressed

An ordinary summer day.

Industry, businesses, and households all draw from the same grid. Demand climbs through the day and peaks in the late afternoon, comfortably under what the system can deliver.

Everyone turns on the AC.

A heatwave pushes demand toward the ceiling. In this grid it just barely fits, with a thin sliver of slack at 5 p.m.

Now connect three data centers.

They draw 1,200 MW day and night, raising the floor under everything else. Without new generation, they remove the slack the system was counting on, so the same heatwave now overshoots the ceiling.

So the operator cuts someone off.

When demand outruns supply, grid operators order controlled outages, called , to keep the whole system from failing. Which raises the essay’s question: if new large loads pushed the system over, should residents be the ones who go dark?

Curtail the large loads first.

Require new data centers to their grid power during extreme weather and other stress events, and to sign the agreement and install the equipment to do it when they connect. Here, trimming the data centers covers the whole shortfall. The neighborhood keeps its lights on.

Curtailing doesn’t have to mean switching off.

Data centers allowed to install their own behind-the-meter generation can step off the grid and keep running on it. Texas now requires large loads to disclose that backup capacity, and lets ERCOT tell them to use it.

Texans have reason to take this seriously. During Winter Storm Uri in February 2021, ERCOT ordered up to 20,000 MW of firm load shed at the worst point of the crisis, according to the FERC–NERC review. SB6’s answer for large loads comes in two parts.

Texas SB6 · §4 · Utilities Code §39.170(a)
Sec. 39.170. LARGE LOAD DEMAND MANAGEMENT SERVICE. (a) The commission shall require the independent organization certified under Section 39.151 for the ERCOT power region to ensure that each electric cooperative, transmission and distribution utility, and municipally owned utility serving a transmission-voltage customer develops a protocol, including the installation of any necessary equipment or technology before the customer is interconnected, to allow the load to be curtailed during firm load shed. The electric cooperative, transmission and distribution utility, or municipally owned utility shall confer with the customer to the extent feasible to shed load in a coordinated manner.

Texas SB6 · §2 · Utilities Code §37.0561(e)
(e) The standards must require each interconnected large load customer subject to Subsection (c) to disclose to the interconnecting electric utility or municipally owned utility information about the customer’s on-site backup generating facilities and require the interconnecting electric utility or municipally owned utility to provide the information to the independent organization certified under Section 39.151 for the ERCOT power region. For the purposes of this subsection, “on-site backup generating facilities” means generation that is not capable of exporting energy to the ERCOT transmission grid and that, in the aggregate, can serve at least 50 percent of on-site demand. The independent organization shall establish a threshold before or during an energy emergency alert at which the organization may issue reasonable notice that large load customers with on-site backup generating facilities may be directed to either deploy the customer’s on-site backup generating facilities or curtail load.

Load shedding is a choice operators make on purpose. It is the controlled alternative to an uncontrolled collapse. The policy question is who sits at the front of the line to be cut.

Texas carves out exceptions: “critical load industrial customers,” for whom losing power would create a dangerous or life-threatening condition on site, and critical natural gas facilities are partly exempt from curtailment.6

Who pays?

The costs of maintaining the grid, upgrading it, and generating electricity are generally borne by , the people who pay an electric bill every month.

In everyday political language, “ratepayers” usually means households. Large loads can raise their costs in two ways: (1) by requiring grid upgrades that everybody pays for, and (2) by increasing demand on supply, which drives up the cost of electricity itself.

Make large loads pay to connect

Require them to cover the cost of their own interconnection, instead of folding it into everyone’s rates.

The hookup bill

Illustrative model: a $250 million upgrade, a utility with 1 million customer accounts, costs recovered over 20 years and split evenly per account. When costs are shared, the data center pays 20% as one large customer among many.

Texas SB6 · §1 · Utilities Code §36 (c-1), (c-2)
(c-1) The commission by rule shall ensure that a large load customer who is subject to the standards adopted under Section 37.0561 contributes to the recovery of the interconnecting electric utility’s costs to interconnect the large load to the utility’s system. (c-2) An electric cooperative or municipally owned utility that has not adopted customer choice shall pass through to a large load customer who is subject to the standards adopted under Section 37.0561 the reasonable costs to interconnect the large load in a manner determined by the electric cooperative or municipally owned utility.

Charge them a rate that fits their use

Put large loads on a special rate, or , or make sure they are charged in a way commensurate with what they use. Done well, it might even let a utility lower rates for everyone else.

New York has already lived through a version of the second problem. Upstate, a wave of crypto-mining operations moved into towns whose municipal utilities buy cheap hydropower.7 Each town gets a fixed allocation of it from the New York Power Authority. Once a town uses it up, it buys pricier supplemental power, and the cost is recovered from all customers.

4CP, 12CP, and dodging the peak

ERCOT splits transmission costs using : each utility’s share depends on its demand during the single highest 15-minute system-peak interval in each of June, July, August, and September. Large customers on demand-based rates are charged the same way.

That creates a game. A flexible large load that can predict those four summer peaks can curtail for a few intervals a year and avoid most of its transmission charges, shifting them onto everyone else. In June 2026, PUCT staff proposed replacing 4CP with 12CP, one peak interval per month, and charging large loads a minimum bill. A revised version advanced in July.

Illustrative model: a 500 MW data center on a system with $1 billion a year in transmission costs. Bars show each month’s peak interval. The data center can call the summer peaks every time and other months’ peaks about half the time. Rule details: PUCT Project 58000, staff memo of June 12, 2026 and revised proposal of July 9, 2026.

New York’s plan

New York’s answer to phantom loads, grid management, and cost allocation is Governor Hochul’s Energize NY Development initiative, announced in her January 2026 State of the State address.

The is carrying it out as a , the vehicle it uses for policymaking. In the governor’s office’s words, the proceeding “will review interconnection processes, cost-allocation mechanisms and tariff structures relating to the integration of large loads with the State’s transmission and distribution systems,” with the goal of supporting economic development “without adversely impacting ratepayers.”

This is a kind of administrative law, but proceedings mostly live in the PSC’s uncodified , a bit like a court’s case law. The PSC does issue rules that land in the state’s book of administrative law, Title 16 NYCRR, but that isn’t the whole picture. You can read the case file, including the order that started it (item 76) and public comments.
